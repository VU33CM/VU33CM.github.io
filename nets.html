📡 Indian Amateur Radio Nets

The heartbeat of the amateur-radio community.

A radio net is more than a list of callsigns and frequencies. It is a scheduled meeting on the air where radio amateurs check in, exchange information, practise operating skills, test their stations, make friends and keep the community connected.

Start here

What exactly is a radio net?

If you are completely new to amateur radio, think of a net as a scheduled group conversation on radio. One operator, called the Net Control Station (NCS), coordinates the conversation and gives other stations an opportunity to check in.

A net may be a relaxed morning gathering, a technical discussion, a regional community net, an emergency-communication exercise, a digital-radio meeting or a specialised activity. Some nets are highly organised; others are informal.

The important idea is simple: stations meet at an agreed time and frequency or system, listen first, and take turns communicating.

In India, current listings cover HF, VHF, UHF, DMR and satellite activity. The published schedules include nets from many states and cities, demonstrating how widely the activity is spread across the amateur-radio community.

Why it matters

Why do radio amateurs check into nets?

Nets combine social communication with practical radio skills. A few minutes on a net can tell an operator a surprising amount about propagation, equipment and the local radio community.

👋

Mark your presence

A regular check-in lets other operators know that you are active on the air and available to communicate.

🌐

Build the community

Nets bring operators together across cities, states and sometimes countries. Familiar callsigns gradually become familiar voices.

📶

Study propagation

Hearing which stations are getting through can provide useful real-world information about current propagation.

🔧

Check your station

Nets can help an operator notice problems with audio, antenna performance, modulation, signal strength or operating technique.

🎙️

Improve operating skills

Regular check-ins build confidence in calling, listening, identifying stations and following proper operating procedure.

🆘

Support preparedness

Well-practised operators are better prepared to communicate efficiently when organised emergency or public-service communications are required.

Operating procedure

How do you check into a net?

The process is simple. The most important rule is: listen first. Every net has its own rhythm and the Net Control Station determines how stations are recognised.

1

Find the net

Confirm the day, time, frequency, repeater or digital system before transmitting.

2

Listen

Listen for the NCS and understand the check-in procedure before you transmit.

3

Wait your turn

Do not interrupt an ongoing exchange. Wait until the NCS requests additional check-ins.

4

Give your callsign

Clearly state your callsign when invited by the Net Control Station.

5

Follow the net

Keep your transmission concise and follow the instructions given by the NCS.

Your first check-in

What should a beginner actually say?

You do not need a long introduction. A simple, clear callsign is often enough to start.

Suppose the NCS says: “Any additional stations wishing to check in?”

You can simply say your callsign clearly, for example:

“VU33CM”

The NCS may acknowledge you and invite you to speak. You can then give your name, location and a short message, depending on the net's normal procedure.

If you are new, it is perfectly reasonable to say that you are a first-time participant. Most community nets are an excellent place to learn proper operating habits by listening.

Good operating

Net etiquette: simple rules that make the airwaves better

✓ Do this

  • Listen before transmitting.
  • Use your complete callsign clearly.
  • Keep transmissions reasonably short.
  • Follow the NCS instructions.
  • Leave space between transmissions.
  • Be polite to new and experienced operators.
  • Give useful information when requested.
  • Move longer technical discussions away from a busy net when appropriate.

⚠ Avoid this

  • Do not transmit over another station.
  • Do not repeatedly call without listening.
  • Do not monopolise the net.
  • Do not use the net frequency for unrelated arguments.
  • Do not assume that an old frequency is still active.
  • Do not ignore the NCS procedure.
  • Do not treat published schedules as permanently fixed.
  • Never transmit outside your authorised privileges.

Reading the table

How to understand a net listing

🕐

IST

Indian Standard Time. Make sure your radio schedule and clock are aligned with IST.

📻

Frequency

The published operating frequency. On repeaters, you may also need the correct offset and tone.

↕️

+ / −

On repeater listings, this commonly indicates the transmit offset direction. Check the current repeater information before transmitting.

🎧

Mode

Examples include LSB, USB, CW and digital systems such as DMR.

📡

Repeater

A repeater receives your signal and retransmits it, extending local VHF/UHF coverage.

🛰️

Satellite

Satellite nets use an amateur satellite or transponder rather than a normal terrestrial repeater.

Operate legally

Nets are part of amateur radio — and amateur radio is regulated

In India, amateur-radio operation is not an unregulated public radio service. Under the Telecommunications (Amateur Services) Rules, 2024 , an amateur station may not be installed or operated except under the terms and conditions of an Amateur Station Operator Certificate (ASOC).

The Department of Telecommunications states that the ASOC supports self-training, intercommunication and technical investigation, and provides for General and Restricted categories.

Therefore, the net tables on this page are intended primarily for licensed/authorised amateur-radio operators. If you are not yet licensed, you can learn, listen where legally permitted and prepare for the hobby — but do not transmit on amateur frequencies without the required authorisation.

Your first net

A simple path for a new operator

You do not need to master every mode or every Q-code before joining your first net.

01 · Learn

Understand your radio

Learn frequency, mode, repeater offset, tone and basic operating controls.

02 · Listen

Find a local net

Listen to one or two sessions before attempting your first check-in.

03 · Check in

Give your callsign

When invited by NCS, transmit your callsign clearly and wait for further instructions.

04 · Return

Become a regular

Regular participation is where the real value appears: friendships, experience and confidence grow over time.

Turn your radio on. Listen. Learn. Check in.

Amateur radio becomes much more enjoyable when you stop thinking of the radio as a piece of equipment and start seeing it as a doorway into a living community of people, technology and experimentation.
